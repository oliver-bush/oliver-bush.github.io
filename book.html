Abandoning and Rediscovering the Treasury View: The Fiscal Foundations of Britain’s Great Inflation

With Michael D. Bordo and Ryland Thomas  ·  Book project in development

Why did Britain, historically one of the world’s most inflation-resistant economies, experience sustained inflation above that of its peers from the late 1960s to the early 1980s? Existing explanations emphasise adverse shocks and monetary-policy mistakes. This book develops a different account: Britain’s Great Inflation followed a deep break in its fiscal-financing regime.

For much of modern British history, the ‘Treasury View’ required public debt to be credibly backed by future primary surpluses. Debt could rise in wartime, but governments were expected to stabilise and reduce it in peacetime. During the post-war transition to demand management and full employment, that commitment weakened. After the collapse of Bretton Woods, fiscal expansion proceeded without an effective nominal anchor, and inflation became part of the mechanism through which public liabilities were stabilised. The book traces the creation, abandonment and eventual restoration of this regime from the early eighteenth century to the adoption of fiscal rules and inflation targeting in the 1990s.

The project combines archival and narrative evidence with quantitative and counterfactual analysis. It places Britain’s experience in comparative perspective and connects the history to modern theories of fiscal inflation and monetary–fiscal interaction.
